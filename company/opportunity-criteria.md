# Opportunity Evaluation Criteria

## Purpose

This document defines the framework used to evaluate potential business opportunities during the company's Exploration Stage.

The framework exists to help the company compare opportunities consistently, identify important weaknesses, and determine where additional research is required.

The framework is a **decision-support tool**.

It is not a decision-making algorithm.

A high score does not authorize pursuing an opportunity, and a low score does not automatically eliminate one.

The Owner makes all final decisions.

---

# 1. Evaluation Process

Opportunities should progress through three stages:

### Stage 1 — Initial Screening

Determine whether enough evidence exists to justify additional research.

### Stage 2 — Opportunity Assessment

Research and score the opportunity using the criteria defined below.

### Stage 3 — Validation

For opportunities the Owner chooses to explore further, design inexpensive experiments that test the most important assumptions before significant time or money is committed.

Do not perform Stage 3 experiments without Owner authorization.

---

# 2. Scoring System

Each criterion should normally be scored from **1 to 5**.

**1 — Very Poor**
Strong evidence suggests the opportunity performs badly on this criterion.

**2 — Poor**
Meaningful weaknesses exist.

**3 — Moderate / Uncertain**
The opportunity appears acceptable, or available evidence is mixed.

**4 — Good**
Evidence suggests the opportunity performs well.

**5 — Excellent**
Strong evidence suggests the opportunity performs exceptionally well.

When insufficient evidence exists, use:

**U — Unknown**

Do not convert uncertainty into an artificial numerical score.

An opportunity containing several important `U` ratings should normally trigger additional research rather than a confident recommendation.

Every score should include a short explanation and supporting evidence where appropriate.

---

# 3. Core Evaluation Criteria

## 3.1 Problem Evidence

**Question:**

Does a real, recurring problem exist?

Look for evidence such as:

* Repeated customer complaints.
* Inefficient workflows.
* Manual work.
* Spreadsheet-based processes.
* Custom scripts.
* Multiple tools being combined to accomplish one task.
* Existing paid solutions.
* Requests for missing functionality.
* Significant time or money being spent on the problem.

### High Score

Strong independent evidence demonstrates that an identifiable group repeatedly experiences the problem.

### Low Score

The problem is primarily hypothetical or appears to exist only because a product idea was invented first.

---

## 3.2 Problem Severity

**Question:**

How much does the problem matter to the affected customer?

Consider:

* Time lost.
* Money lost.
* Frustration.
* Frequency.
* Business consequences.
* Opportunity cost.
* Difficulty of existing workarounds.

Prefer problems customers actively want solved over minor inconveniences.

---

## 3.3 Willingness to Pay

**Question:**

Is there evidence that customers will spend money to solve this problem?

Evidence may include:

* Existing paid competitors.
* Subscription services.
* Commercial tools.
* Customers hiring people to perform the work.
* Paid workarounds.
* Purchasing behavior in adjacent categories.

Statements such as:

> "I wish something like this existed."

are weak evidence of willingness to pay.

Actual spending behavior is much stronger evidence.

---

## 3.4 Customer Identifiability

**Question:**

Can we clearly identify who the customer is?

A strong opportunity should allow us to describe the likely customer specifically enough that future research can determine where they gather, what they use, and how they purchase.

"Everyone" is not an identifiable customer.

---

## 3.5 Customer Reachability

**Question:**

Can a small company realistically reach these customers?

Consider:

* Search behavior.
* Online communities.
* Professional communities.
* Marketplaces.
* App stores.
* Social platforms.
* Industry publications.
* Conferences.
* Existing audiences.
* Partnerships.
* Directories.
* Organic discovery.

A valuable product with no affordable path to customers may still be a poor opportunity.

---

## 3.6 Competitive Environment

**Question:**

How difficult would it be to compete?

Competition is not automatically negative.

Existing successful competitors may provide valuable evidence that customers spend money in the category.

Evaluate:

* Number of competitors.
* Competitor quality.
* Pricing.
* Customer loyalty.
* Switching costs.
* Market concentration.
* Customer dissatisfaction.
* Missing features.
* Poorly served customer segments.
* Distribution advantages.
* Brand advantages.

### High Score

Demand is validated, but meaningful room appears to exist for differentiation or an underserved segment.

### Low Score

Strong incumbents already solve the problem extremely well and possess substantial advantages that a small entrant cannot reasonably overcome.

---

## 3.7 Differentiation Potential

**Question:**

Could the company offer a meaningful reason for customers to choose its solution?

Differentiation may come from:

* Better usability.
* Specialization.
* Better workflow integration.
* Lower complexity.
* Better automation.
* Better customer experience.
* Different pricing.
* Privacy.
* Ownership/local operation.
* Better interoperability.
* Serving an overlooked niche.

"Uses AI" is not sufficient differentiation by itself.

---

## 3.8 Initial Build Feasibility

**Question:**

Can a useful initial version be created with the resources available to a very small company?

Consider:

* Technical complexity.
* Required expertise.
* Development time.
* Infrastructure.
* Data requirements.
* Licensing.
* Hardware.
* Capital requirements.
* Third-party dependencies.

Prefer opportunities where a meaningful initial product can be tested before building a large system.

---

## 3.9 Validation Cost

**Question:**

How cheaply can the most important assumptions be tested?

A high score means meaningful market evidence can be obtained through inexpensive experiments.

A low score means substantial money or development effort must be committed before learning whether customers care.

Prefer opportunities that allow the company to **buy information cheaply**.

---

## 3.10 Operating Burden

**Question:**

How difficult will the business be to operate after the product exists?

Consider:

* Customer support.
* Content moderation.
* Manual processing.
* Sales requirements.
* Account management.
* Infrastructure maintenance.
* Data maintenance.
* Legal/compliance work.
* Refunds/disputes.
* Continuous content creation.
* Vendor management.

### High Score

The business can operate with relatively little recurring manual intervention.

### Low Score

Revenue growth requires approximately proportional growth in human labor.

---

## 3.11 Automation Leverage

**Question:**

How much of the business can responsibly be assisted or operated through software and AI agents?

Potential areas include:

* Research.
* Development.
* Testing.
* Documentation.
* Customer support preparation.
* Marketing analysis.
* Operations.
* Monitoring.
* Reporting.
* Content preparation.
* Data processing.

A high score does not require eliminating humans.

It means automation can meaningfully increase the productive capacity of a very small organization.

---

## 3.12 Revenue Scalability

**Question:**

Can revenue increase without requiring a proportional increase in the Owner's working hours?

Examples with potentially strong scalability include:

* Software.
* Digital products.
* Games.
* Licensing.
* Subscriptions.
* Marketplaces.
* Reusable intellectual property.

Service businesses may still qualify if substantial portions of delivery can be productized or automated.

---

## 3.13 Asset Creation

**Question:**

Does pursuing this opportunity create durable assets for the company?

Possible assets include:

* Software.
* Intellectual property.
* Proprietary data.
* Brand recognition.
* Distribution channels.
* Audience.
* Research.
* Reusable technology.
* Automated systems.
* Content libraries.
* Business processes.

Prefer opportunities where work performed today can continue creating value tomorrow.

---

## 3.14 Owner Fit

**Question:**

Does this opportunity fit the Owner's capabilities, interests, working style, and desired future?

Consider:

* Existing technical skills.
* Existing domain knowledge.
* Genuine curiosity about the problem.
* Ability to evaluate the quality of work produced by AI agents or contractors.
* Desire to spend significant time in the market.
* Compatibility with other responsibilities.
* Type of work required after the business succeeds.

Do not assume that an opportunity fits merely because it involves software, games, AI, or another subject the Owner has previously explored.

When Owner Fit cannot be determined from available company documentation, mark it `U` rather than making assumptions about the Owner.

An important question is:

> **If this business succeeds, would the Owner actually want the job that success creates?**

---

## 3.15 Time to Value

**Question:**

How quickly could the company reasonably reach meaningful validation, its first customer, or its first revenue?

The company does not require immediate revenue from every project.

However, opportunities requiring years of investment before meaningful validation should receive additional scrutiny.

---

## 3.16 Capital Requirements

**Question:**

How much money must be committed before the opportunity can be meaningfully tested?

Prefer opportunities that can begin with modest financial commitments.

Large capital requirements are not automatically disqualifying, but they should require stronger evidence and explicit Owner approval.

---

## 3.17 Dependency Risk

**Question:**

How dependent would the business be on organizations or technologies outside the company's control?

Consider dependencies on:

* AI model providers.
* Cloud platforms.
* App stores.
* Social networks.
* Search engines.
* APIs.
* Marketplaces.
* Payment processors.
* Data providers.
* Licensing agreements.

Evaluate what happens if a provider:

* Raises prices.
* Changes its API.
* Restricts access.
* Changes platform rules.
* Introduces a competing product.
* Shuts down.

Prefer businesses where critical dependencies can eventually be replaced or mitigated.

---

## 3.18 AI Commoditization Risk

**Question:**

Could improving general-purpose AI make this product unnecessary or trivial to reproduce?

Consider whether customers could eventually obtain essentially the same result by asking a general-purpose AI assistant.

### High Score

AI improves the company's ability to deliver the product or strengthens its competitive advantage.

### Low Score

The product's primary value could easily become a standard feature of general-purpose AI systems.

Do not assume that adding AI to a product protects it from AI disruption.

---

## 3.19 Defensibility

**Question:**

If the opportunity succeeds, what becomes increasingly difficult for competitors to reproduce?

Potential sources include:

* Proprietary data.
* Customer relationships.
* Brand.
* Community.
* Network effects.
* Specialized expertise.
* Integrations.
* Workflow adoption.
* Intellectual property.
* Distribution.
* Accumulated content.
* Operational excellence.

Not every small business requires a powerful competitive moat.

However, the Analyst should identify what, if anything, becomes stronger as the business grows.

---

## 3.20 Legal, Regulatory, and Reputation Risk

**Question:**

Does the opportunity introduce meaningful legal, regulatory, ethical, privacy, security, or reputational concerns?

Potential issues include:

* Personal data.
* Financial information.
* Medical information.
* Children's data.
* Copyright.
* Licensing.
* User-generated content.
* Professional advice.
* Regulated industries.
* Security-sensitive systems.

Significant concerns should be clearly flagged regardless of the overall opportunity score.

The Analyst should identify potential concerns but should not present itself as providing legal advice.

---

# 4. Critical Criteria

The following criteria deserve particular attention:

* Problem Evidence
* Willingness to Pay
* Customer Reachability
* Initial Build Feasibility
* Operating Burden
* Owner Fit
* AI Commoditization Risk

A strong total score should **not** hide a serious weakness in one of these areas.

For example, a technically easy product with no evidence of customer demand remains a weak opportunity.

---

# 5. Opportunity Scorecard

Research reports evaluating a specific opportunity should include a scorecard similar to:

| Criterion                            | Score | Confidence        | Evidence / Reasoning |
| ------------------------------------ | ----: | ----------------- | -------------------- |
| Problem Evidence                     | 1–5/U | Low/Moderate/High |                      |
| Problem Severity                     | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Willingness to Pay                   | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Customer Identifiability             | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Customer Reachability                | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Competitive Environment              | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Differentiation Potential            | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Initial Build Feasibility            | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Validation Cost                      | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Operating Burden                     | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Automation Leverage                  | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Revenue Scalability                  | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Asset Creation                       | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Owner Fit                            | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Time to Value                        | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Capital Requirements                 | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Dependency Risk                      | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| AI Commoditization Risk              | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Defensibility                        | 1–5/U |                   |                      |
| Legal / Regulatory / Reputation Risk | 1–5/U |                   |                      |

Do not calculate a simple total and treat it as the final answer.

Different criteria matter differently depending on the opportunity.

Use the scorecard to expose strengths, weaknesses, uncertainty, and tradeoffs.

# 6. Red Flags

The Analyst should prominently flag opportunities exhibiting characteristics such as:

* No evidence of a recurring problem.
* Customers expect the solution to be free.
* Customer acquisition appears unusually difficult or expensive.
* The market depends primarily on hype.
* Significant development is required before demand can be tested.
* Success requires substantial manual labor from the Owner.
* Revenue scales primarily by increasing working hours.
* The product can easily be replicated using a general-purpose AI assistant.
* A single external platform effectively controls the business.
* Legal or regulatory exposure is substantial.
* The opportunity requires significant capital before validation.
* The Owner would need to perform work they may not want to perform long term.
* Existing competitors solve the problem extremely well with high customer satisfaction.

Red flags do not automatically eliminate an opportunity.

They identify assumptions requiring stronger evidence.

---

# 7. Green Flags

Characteristics that may justify deeper investigation include:

* Customers already pay for imperfect solutions.
* Customers repeatedly construct their own workarounds.
* Multiple competitors validate demand but share recurring customer complaints.
* A clearly identifiable niche is poorly served.
* A useful MVP can be built cheaply.
* Demand can be tested before substantial development.
* Customers are concentrated in reachable communities or channels.
* Automation substantially reduces operating costs.
* Revenue can grow without proportional Owner labor.
* Work creates reusable or appreciating assets.
* The Owner has an unusual combination of relevant skills or knowledge.
* AI strengthens the business rather than replacing its core value.
* Early customers can be served before the product is fully developed.

---

# 8. Opportunity Recommendation

After evaluating an opportunity, the Analyst should assign one of the following recommendations:

### Reject

Available evidence indicates the opportunity should not receive additional company resources.

### Monitor

The opportunity is not currently compelling, but future changes may justify revisiting it.

### Research Further

The opportunity appears potentially interesting, but important questions remain unanswered.

### Validate

Enough evidence exists to recommend designing a small real-world experiment.

### Consider Pursuit

Validation evidence suggests the opportunity may justify a larger commitment.

Only the Owner can authorize moving from research into validation or from validation into active pursuit.

---

# 9. Required Conclusion

Every completed opportunity assessment should answer:

1. **What problem appears to exist?**
2. **What is the strongest evidence that it matters?**
3. **What is the strongest evidence that customers will pay?**
4. **Why are existing solutions insufficient?**
5. **Why might this company be able to compete?**
6. **What is the strongest argument against pursuing it?**
7. **What important information is still unknown?**
8. **What is the cheapest next experiment that would reduce the most important uncertainty?**
9. **What does the Analyst recommend doing next?**
10. **How confident is the Analyst in that recommendation?**

---

# 10. Guiding Principle

> **An opportunity becomes more valuable as uncertainty decreases — not as enthusiasm increases.**

Research should help the company spend small amounts of time and money to avoid wasting large amounts of time and money.
